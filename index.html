A quick reference for how fantasy football works, common terms you'll hear on draft day, and how to use this app's tools well. Your league's exact rules can vary — when in doubt, check your league's settings or ask your commissioner.

The Basics

How the game works

You draft a roster of real NFL players, then each week your starting lineup earns points based on those players' real-game stats (yards, touchdowns, receptions, etc). You're matched up against another team in your league each week — most points wins. Most leagues run a regular season through roughly Week 14, then a playoff bracket among the top teams for the final weeks.

Scoring formats

The biggest variable between leagues is how receptions are scored:

  • Standard — no points for catches, only yards and touchdowns.
  • PPR (Points Per Reception) — +1 point every time a player catches a pass. Boosts pass-catching RBs, WRs, and TEs significantly.
  • Half-PPR — +0.5 per catch, a common middle ground.

Typical baseline scoring: 1 pt / 25 passing yards, 4 pts / passing TD, 1 pt / 10 rushing or receiving yards, 6 pts / rushing or receiving TD, −2 for an interception or lost fumble. Kickers and defenses use their own scales (distance-based for field goals; sacks, turnovers, and points allowed for defense). Confirm your exact league settings — this app's rankings assume a PPR-style format.

Roster positions
  • QB, RB, WR, TE — standard starting slots for each position.
  • FLEX — can be filled by an RB, WR, or TE (whichever you have the best matchup/depth at). Some leagues also use Superflex, which allows a QB in that slot too — this makes QBs far more valuable to draft early.
  • DST — your team defense/special teams as a unit; scores on sacks, turnovers, safeties, and defensive/return touchdowns.
  • K — kicker; scores on field goals (more for longer ones) and extra points.
  • Bench — reserve players not in your starting lineup this week. This is where depth and bye-week insurance live.
  • IR — some leagues offer an Injured Reserve slot that doesn't count against your roster limit, only usable for players the NFL has officially designated injured (not just "questionable").

Draft Day

Snake vs. auction drafts

Most leagues use a snake draft: pick order reverses each round (if you pick 3rd in round 1, you pick 3rd-from-last in round 2), so everyone gets a similar number of early picks over the whole draft. Some leagues instead run an auction draft, where everyone gets a budget (e.g. $200) to bid on any player, and you build your whole roster within that budget.

ADP, rank, and "value" — what this app's Value column means

ADP (Average Draft Position) is where a player is actually getting picked across real drafts. Rank (or ECR, Expert Consensus Ranking) is where analysts think a player should go based on projected performance. The Value column on the Draft Board is the gap between those two: a green +N means experts rate the player better than where they're being drafted — a bargain if they're still on the board. A red −N means the opposite — the market's drafting them ahead of their expert value, i.e. a likely reach.

Tiers and positional scarcity

Players are often grouped into tiers — clusters of roughly equal value with a real drop-off ("cliff") between one tier and the next. Rather than just chasing the single top-ranked name, it's often smarter to grab the last good player in a tier before it disappears. Some positions (historically tight end) get thin fast after the top options are gone — that's positional scarcity, and it's exactly what the Best Available panel and its "top-tier left" counter on the Draft Board tab are built to surface.

Handcuffing

Drafting your own star running back's backup as a late-round insurance pick. If the starter gets hurt, you already own the player likely to inherit the touches — instead of scrambling on the waiver wire against everyone else in your league.

Using Team Needs to draft a balanced roster

The Your Team Needs chips on the Draft Board track how many players you've drafted at each position against typical roster-construction targets (enough for a realistic bench, not just your starting lineup). A chip turns green once you've hit that target — a quick gut-check against accidentally drafting five wide receivers and forgetting kicker entirely.

Managing Your Team During the Season

Bye weeks

Every NFL team gets one week off during the season — none of their players score any points that week. This app flags bye weeks automatically on the Weekly Lineup tab (and on your season roster), so you can see the conflict before you're stuck without a starter.

Injury statuses
  • Questionable (Q) — uncertain, roughly a coin flip; worth checking closer to kickoff.
  • Doubtful (D) — unlikely to play.
  • Out (O) — confirmed not playing that week.
  • IR — on Injured Reserve, out for an extended period.

Set these on the Weekly Lineup or My Team bench tables as news breaks, and the app flags any starter with a non-Healthy status.

Auto-Optimize, explained

The ⚡ Auto-Optimize Lineup button on Weekly Lineup rebuilds that week's starting lineup from scratch: any player on bye or marked Out/Doubtful/IR gets bumped for your best available healthy option at that position, ranked by who's actually best (not just first-available). It only touches that specific week — your season-long default roster plan on the My Team tab is untouched, so you don't have to manually revert anything the following week.

Waiver wire & free agents

Players not on anyone's roster are free agents; if someone drops a player or a breakout emerges, they usually go through a waiver period first, awarded by waiver priority or a bidding system called FAAB (Free Agent Acquisition Budget — everyone gets a season-long budget to bid on adds). This app doesn't manage waivers directly, but the Draft Board's search/filter works well as a quick way to check a free agent's rank and value before you bid.

Trades

Most leagues allow trading players directly with other teams, usually with a trade deadline a few weeks before the playoffs. A good trade target: sell a player whose value is unusually high right now ("sell high"), or buy a good player who's temporarily struggling ("buy low") — both often visible as a big gap between their reputation and their current form.
